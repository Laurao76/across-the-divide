I kept hearing that America was becoming more politically divided. I wanted to understand what that actually looks like in the data, and what it might mean for a more thoughtful news experience.

THE QUESTIONS THAT STARTED THIS PROJECT

How have Americans’ feelings toward the opposing party changed from 2000 to 2024 as news pathways shifted?

I examined feelings toward both parties across four elections, then explored today’s news environment.

  • How do news-use pathways differ by partisan group in 2024, and how are they associated with partisan rating gaps?
  • Which issues and concerns do people raise about the opposing party?
  • How might a product that deliberately presents multiple viewpoints encourage more intentional news consumption?

The first questions guide the analysis. The product question motivates a future experiment.

THE CENTRAL FINDING

The gap grew.
Feelings toward the other party got colder.

Across the four elections I examined, partisan rating gaps widened for both groups. Much of that widening came from lower ratings of the opposing party, while ratings of one’s own party were comparatively stable.

INITIAL EXPLORATORY DATA ANALYSIS

First, get a sense of the divide.

My initial EDA was about understanding the data before deciding what to explore more deeply. I started with the historical partisan rating gap: each respondent’s own-party rating minus their opposing-party rating.

WHAT THIS MEANS

The partisan rating gap increased for both Democratic- and Republican-leaning respondents between 2000 and 2024.

Those questions became the basis for my later dashboard. At this initial stage, I also wanted to understand how people encountered news in 2024, and whether differences in news-use frequency lined up with differences in partisan feelings.

WHAT THIS MEANS

News-use patterns vary by partisan group, although the differences are modest for several pathways. Democratic-leaning respondents report more frequent use of online news and especially newspaper news, while Republican-leaning respondents report slightly more cable-news use.

WHAT THIS MEANS

The associations vary depending on the news pathway, frequency of news use, and partisan group. There isn’t one consistent pattern across all of them.

04

FROM ANALYSIS TO A PRODUCT QUESTION

Could a different news experience
make room for understanding?

With a basic understanding of the partisan divide and what it looks like across news pathways, I returned to my product question: How might a product that deliberately presents multiple viewpoints encourage more intentional news consumption?

The existing data cannot tell me whether a particular news experience changes how people view the other side. My proposed A/B test takes a first step by asking whether a multi-viewpoint story increases readers’ openness to opposing perspectives.

PROPOSED EXPERIMENT · NOT YET CONDUCTED
A

A standard news story

A concise, factual article with sources.

B

The same story, plus perspectives

The identical article with an additional “how coverage differs” section presenting clearly sourced viewpoints from the left and the right.

My prediction: adding a multi-viewpoint section will increase openness to opposing political perspectives by making the concerns behind those views more understandable.

PRIMARY OUTCOME

Openness

Survey questions about understanding differing views, recognizing legitimate concerns, and willingness to read a source one disagrees with.

SECONDARY OUTCOME

Out-party warmth

A 0–100 feeling thermometer before and after reading, connecting the study back to the party ratings in this analysis.

GUARDRAILS

A useful reading experience

Check comprehension and enjoyment. Greater openness has limited value if the added text makes the product a chore to use.

What would success look like?

Participants would have 30 minutes to read the article and complete the survey. Example items include whether they better understand why someone might hold a different view, recognize legitimate concerns in people who disagree, and would read another source presenting an opposing perspective.

What could mislead the comparison?

A strongly polarizing topic could prompt people to answer from existing beliefs rather than the story. Familiar sources could also trigger distrust. I would choose a topic with some room for agreement and use clearly sourced facts and substantiated opinions rather than emotionally charged coverage. These choices would need piloting; they do not eliminate the risks.

How would I decide what to do next?

I would compare openness, check out-party warmth, review results separately by party, and examine missing responses, comprehension, and enjoyment. If the additional section helped understanding but made reading unpleasant, I would revise it. I would likely test another story with a fresh group before adopting the approach. Findings from a single story may not generalize to other topics or readers, including those with especially entrenched views.

Study design and decisions still to refine

The proposed audience is US voters who identify with or lean toward a party, with a 30-minute reading and survey session. Before running the study, I would finalize random assignment and persistent assignment to A or B, the recruitment endpoint, an openness scoring rule, and a meaningful improvement threshold.

The topic and source choices could affect responses. I would review results by party, examine missing responses and comprehension, and consider testing another article with a fresh sample before adoption. More intentional news consumption is the broader ambition; this short study measures immediate openness and warmth, not long-term behavior.

READ THE FINDINGS WITH THESE LIMITS IN MIND

A clearer picture.
Still an incomplete one.

Four election snapshots

The historical analysis uses 2000, 2008, 2016, and 2024. It does not track every intervening year or the same people aging. Survey modes and samples changed over time.

Association, not explanation

News-use comparisons come from 2024. They cannot establish causality, long-term media effects, or the influence of particular outlets.

A defined partisan sample

Pure independents are excluded from the own-/other-party gap because this measure requires an assigned party. Weights improve representation but cannot remove every source of bias.

Themes need interpretation

Open-ended questions explicitly ask about dislikes. Their negative tone is expected; keyword matches may miss nuance, and a shared theme does not mean agreement.

05

WHAT’S NEXT

Take the question beyond the notebook.

Tangle News describes itself as an independent, nonpartisan, subscriber-supported news organization. It presents perspectives from the left, right, and center, making its approach directly relevant to my question about deliberately exposing readers to different viewpoints.

Outreach underway: I’m taking these findings to Tangle to ask for their perspective and whether they have relevant evaluation data about readers’ openness or partisan feelings. Their format makes them a useful conversation partner; it does not itself demonstrate reduced polarization.

WHY TANGLE, FOR ME

I’ve been reading Tangle for two years now as a way to understand how people and news outside my political bubble see things. As a reader, I’ve found it insightful, frustrating, earnest, and authentic, sometimes all at once.

Something I find especially interesting is that I recall Tangle describing readers unsubscribing from the very same newsletter because it was “too left” or “too right.” The same piece of work, read in completely different ways! To me, those conflicting reactions raise a question about how divided we are and how much we might need perspectives outside our own bubbles to bring us closer together.
